Why this, why now

Something is being lost in the first lesson.

In many classrooms in Sikkim, a child's first meeting with English is a letter name: A, B, C — sounds that appear in almost no English word. Some children are taught "B says buh." Many are taught no sounds at all. Either way, the material reading is actually built from — the pure sounds of the language — is missing from the very first lesson.

This is not a failure of effort. Teachers in Sikkim work with extraordinary dedication, often in multiple languages, often without training materials that match their students' reality. The tools have simply not existed for this context.

Mentava's Alphabet Sounds book was created to fix the foundation: the 44 phonemes of English — not the 26 letter names — taught with precision, warmth, and visual richness. Our Phoneme-abet Book builds on it for Sikkim, and leaves space on every page for readers to add words from their own languages, so the sounds belong here from the very first day.

"A book about sounds should leave room for every language in the room."
The cognitive science

Sound first. Everything else follows.

1 44, not 26

English has 44 distinct sounds but only 26 letters. Every traditional alphabet book ignores 18 of them. Mentava's book teaches all 44 — including the two 'th' sounds, the 'sh', the 'ch' — because children need all 44 to read.

2 Sound before name

Children are taught that A is "ay", B is "bee", C is "see." But "cat" is not pronounced see-ay-tee. Learning letter names before letter sounds actively slows reading. Mentava teaches sounds from the start.

3 Pure sounds, pure blending

When a child hears "mmm-aaa-ppp" they can hear "map." When they hear "muh-ah-puh" they cannot. Isolated, pure phoneme sounds — with no trailing vowel — are what make blending possible. This is what the book and this curriculum teach.

Designed for here

Most phonics tools were not made for Sikkim. This curriculum is.

Sikkim's classrooms are multilingual by nature. Children arrive speaking Nepali, Sikkimese, Lepcha, Bhutia — and then meet English. That is a gift, not a problem. But it means the curriculum has to meet teachers and students where they are, not where a textbook imagines they are.

The challenge Some images in the book — a bagel, an astronaut, an alligator — may be unfamiliar to children in rural Sikkim. If a child doesn't know the word, the image can't anchor the sound.
↓
Our response Each phoneme page comes with a bilingual teacher note (English + Nepali) identifying which images may need introduction, and suggesting a familiar local equivalent. A khukuri for axe. Dhido for a food that starts with D. The book's images stay; local knowledge fills the gaps.
The challenge Teachers in Sikkim often learned English phonics the same way their students did — with letter names, not sounds, and with schwa-added pronunciation. You cannot model what you haven't yet heard.
↓
Our response The curriculum leads with teacher preparation, not classroom deployment. Every phoneme has a short audio clip — produced by a native English speaker — that teachers listen to and repeat before teaching. A pronunciation card for each sound is built into the teacher guide. Teachers learn first. Students come second.
The challenge Many Himalayan languages do not have the English /r/ versus /l/ distinction, or the /th/ sounds, or the /v/ versus /w/ contrast. These aren't subtle differences for a native speaker — they are genuinely new sound categories for learners here.
↓
Our response The curriculum's most critical component is a targeted minimal-pairs training sequence for the specific contrasts that are hard from a Nepali phonological background — not a generic English-learner program. The comparisons the book already contains (l vs. r, s vs. th, b vs. v) become the spine of dedicated teacher and student practice modules.
What we are building

Six tools. One coherent system.

Each component is designed around a simple rule: if the teacher can't do it confidently, the student can't learn it. So we start with teachers.

For teachers first
1The Phoneme-abet Book

Our adaptation of Mentava's Alphabet Sounds, made for Sikkim — available online, as a downloadable ebook/PDF, or in physical print. Every phoneme page carries two QR codes: one linking to a 5-second audio clip of the pure sound, produced by a native English speaker — no schwa, no trailing vowel — and one linking to that sound's jingle. Teachers play them on a phone before and during the lesson. Each page also leaves space for readers to add words from their own languages. Works offline after first download. No app required.

2Bilingual teacher guide

A printed companion guide in English and Nepali covering: what phonics is and why it works, how to say each sound correctly, which images on each page may need local context, and how to run a 10-minute phoneme lesson. Written in plain language. Tested with teachers before printing.

3Pronunciation video (Nepali)

A 3-minute video, presented by a Sikkimese educator, explaining the phonics method in Nepali and demonstrating the correct versus incorrect way to produce sounds. Shareable by WhatsApp. The trust-building layer.

Then for students
4Phoneme tap game

A simple web app using the book's images. An audio clip plays a phoneme. The student taps the pictures that begin with that sound. Immediate, warm feedback. Three difficulty levels, moving from easy confirmation to minimal-pair discrimination. Designed for tablets and basic smartphones. Works on very low bandwidth.

5Phoneme songs

Twenty-second jingles — one per sound — built on a single repeating melody. Only the phoneme and example words change. "Say the sound — fff! fff is for fish, fff is for fox, fff is for flower and fork in a box — say the sound — fff!" Pre-recorded, playable from QR, singable in class.

6WhatsApp audio drops for families

Short audio messages in Nepali, shareable by the teacher to families over WhatsApp. Each message covers one phoneme: what it is, how to say it, and a simple home activity parents can do with their child without knowing English. Designed for families who cannot read the book but can support learning.

"A child who hears the right sound at the right moment — and feels the pleasure of producing it correctly — has already taken the first step toward reading. That moment is worth everything we are building here."
Help us build the Phoneme-abet Book

Lend us your words.

The Phoneme-abet Book will be filled with things children in Sikkim already know — not bagels and astronauts, but the words of home. We are collecting local words, from every language spoken here, for objects, foods, animals, and places that children recognize — words that begin with each of the 44 sounds of English. Nepali, Sikkimese, Lepcha, Bhutia, and any language your family speaks: all of them are welcome in this book.

We are also collecting the English words that are genuinely hard for the local ear and tongue — words like film, frisk, or school, where a cluster of sounds or an unfamiliar contrast trips the speaker. Every word you flag becomes a marked challenge in the curriculum, with dedicated listening and practice built around it.

Have a word to contribute? Send it to the project team. Please include the language, the word, and the sound it begins with—or tell us which English word is difficult to hear or say.

A message to teachers

You are not starting from zero. You are starting from exactly the right place.

You already know what it takes to hold a classroom in three languages. You already know how to reach a child who is bored, or frightened, or distracted. What this curriculum adds is one simple thing: a way to make English sounds clear — for yourself first, and then for your students.

You do not need to be a native English speaker. You do not need years of phonics training. You need a phone, a QR code, and a willingness to listen before you teach. The audio clips do the modeling. The guide does the explaining. Your relationship with your students does the rest.

We designed every part of this curriculum assuming that the most important person in the room is you — not the book, not the app, not the songs. Those are tools. You are the teacher.
